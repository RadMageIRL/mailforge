How the terms work

ip4/ip6 authorize addresses and cost no DNS lookups. a, mx, include, exists, ptr and redirect each cost one of the 10 lookups a receiver allows, and included records spend from the same budget.

The qualifier sets the result when a term matches: + pass (default), ~ softfail, - fail, ? neutral. End with ~all or -all; +all authorizes the whole internet.

    Several values can be added at once, separated by spaces or commas. New terms go in before all.

      Help and references
      
      
      
        
      
          
      spf, dkim and dmarc in short
      
          
      SPF (Sender Policy Framework) is a TXT record at your domain that lists the servers allowed to send mail with your domain in the envelope sender (Return-Path). Receivers compare the connecting server's address with that list. Each include, a, mx, exists, ptr and redirect costs a DNS lookup, and the whole record, including everything it includes, may use at most 10. Over the limit, SPF fails for all your mail.
      
          
      DKIM (DomainKeys Identified Mail) signs each message. Your mail system keeps the private key; the public key is published at selector._domainkey.yourdomain so receivers can check the signature. Use RSA keys of 2048 bits; long keys are published as several strings in one TXT record.
      
          
      DMARC ties the two to the address people see. A message passes when SPF or DKIM passes for a domain aligned with the From address. The record at _dmarc.yourdomain tells receivers what to do with mail that fails (none, quarantine or reject) and where to send reports.
      
          
      Troubleshooting usually comes down to one of these: two SPF records at one name, too many SPF lookups, a DKIM key split into two TXT records, a selector that does not match the one your signer uses, or a DMARC record whose first tag is not v=DMARC1. MailForge checks each of them.
      
        
      
        
      
          
      one record, several strings
      
          
      A DNS TXT string holds at most 255 bytes. Longer values, typically DKIM keys and long SPF records, are published as several strings inside one TXT record. Receivers join the strings back together with nothing in between (SPF §3.3, DKIM §3.6.2.2).
      
          
      
            
      ✓ one record, two stringss1._domainkey TXT "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjAN…" "…IDAQAB"
      
            
      ✗ two recordss1._domainkey TXT "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjAN…"
      s1._domainkey TXT "…IDAQAB"
      
          
      
          
      Two records break DKIM (neither half is a valid key) and, for SPF or DMARC, cause a permerror or no policy at all. In the Route 53 console, every line in the Value box is a separate record, so keep all strings on one line.
      
        
      
        
      
          
      ttl guidance
      
          
        
            
      • 3600 (one hour) is a sensible default for all three records.
        • 
            
      • A day before a planned change, lower the TTL to 300 so a mistake can be undone quickly; raise it again once the new record checks out.
        • 
            
      • When rotating DKIM keys, publish the new selector first, switch signing over, and keep the old key published for at least a week so mail already in transit still verifies.
        • 
          
      
        
      
        
      
          
      what "valid" means here
      
          
      MailForge checks syntax, limits and known pitfalls against the RFCs, and live DNS when you inspect. It cannot know whether the servers listed in SPF are really yours, whether your mail server signs with the private key matching the DKIM key, or how a particular receiver treats your mail. The test message in the after-publishing checklist is the final word.
      
          
      Proposed repairs are only applied when you choose them. Each change is labeled: format same meaning, no effect removes something receivers never evaluate, merge combines address ranges covering exactly the same addresses, fix changes how receivers behave (for example a record they currently ignore). by hand items need your decision.
      
        
      
        
      
          
      privacy
      
          
      Building and checking happen in this page. Nothing is uploaded and there is no account, analytics or tracking. Your work is saved in this browser's local storage so it survives a reload.
      
          
      Inspect, "Fetch published" and "Check published" are the only parts that use the network. They send the DNS names being looked up (such as _dmarc.example.com) to Cloudflare (cloudflare-dns.com) or Google (dns.google) over HTTPS, the same lookups any mail server would do. The records you type are never sent, and the page asks the browser not to send its address with those requests. Everything else works offline.
